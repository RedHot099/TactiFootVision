Ball localisation in broadcast football video

Jakub Eichner · TactiFoot Vision · research summary, October 2026

How often can a broadcast frame place the ball on the pitch, within 5 m of where it is? The ball is the hardest object in the scene. It is often under 12 pixels, blurred, hidden by players or in the air.

We find where a strong single-frame detector fails and test one remedy at a time, under a protocol fixed before results are read. Temporal tracking in pitch coordinates gives the largest gain.

Higher resolution, fine-tuning, a second-stage classifier and tracker refinements add nothing measurable on top of it. Below about 1 m the measure is limited by noise in the reference annotation.

1  Problem, data and measure

The question is where the ball is on the pitch, in metres. Figure 1 shows the pipeline that answers it best.

  • Measure. The share of frames whose predicted ball lies within 5 m of the annotated ball, both projected with the frame's homography. A missed ball, a ball on the wrong object and a frame without a homography all count as failures.
  • Data. SoccerNet Game State Reconstruction (GSR) has 30-second broadcast clips at 25 fps with ball positions and camera calibration. Validation has 58 clips from 3 games, test 49 clips from 3 other games.
  • Baseline. Our retrained people-and-ball detector, YOLO11l at 1280 px, with ball AP@50 0.46. Its most confident ball per frame is the reference for every paired comparison.
  • Protocol. We choose variants on two validation games and confirm once on the third. Each experiment has one primary comparison, fixed before its result is read. CIs are 95% bootstraps over whole clips.

2  Where a single-frame detector fails

Where does a strong single-frame detector fail? Mostly on small balls, and on referees in black that it calls “ball”.

Half of all misses are balls smaller than 12 px. Motion blur explains only 19%.

Referees and assistants in black kits cause 52% of confident false balls and 55% of placements more than 5 m off. Their boxes are 46–141 px long. A true ball is never longer than 43 px.

A size filter at 45 px therefore removes them at no cost. On its own it halves the share of placements more than 5 m off, from 18.5% to 9.6%.

3  What was tested

Which remedy places more balls? Only temporal tracking cleared the planned target, and it held on the held-out game and on the test split.

At 1920 px the detector finds more balls, but also more confident false ones. Fine-tuning on oversampled hard balls changed nothing (+0.3 pp). An epoch without mosaic augmentation on a restarted schedule cost 8.5 pp.

Two instructive negatives: tiling and synthetic data

Tiling. The detector runs on four overlapping 1024×608 tiles at 1024 px and on the full frame at 1280 px. Their balls are merged, people come from the full frame, and the 45 px filter runs after the merge.

On single frames tiling helps. On the held-out game it adds +5.29 pp [+3.15, +8.02], from 77.1% to 82.4%. On the whole validation split it adds +6.03 pp [+4.71, +7.39], and ball AP50 rises from 0.356 to 0.397.

The filter is what makes tiling work. Without it, tiles lose 0.73 pp on the two selection games, because the extra resolution mostly adds large false balls.

On top of tracking the gain does not hold. On the held-out game tiles change the tracked result by −1.25 pp [−2.79, +0.24] and add placements more than 5 m off, +2.48 pp [+1.15, +4.08].

On the selection games they add +4.78 pp [+1.55, +8.39]. The tracker already recovers most weak or missed balls that tiles find, and then follows the false balls they bring.

Tiles cost 3.8× the detection time, 75–87 ms per frame against 19.6 ms. Balls under 8 px stay missed, with recall about 0.06. Tiling is a single-frame mode, not part of the video pipeline.

Synthetic data. SoccerSynth has 50,877 rendered images. Only the ball and people are labelled, so we masked the goalkeeper and referee classes out of the classification loss.

We fine-tuned a real-data checkpoint on SoccerSynth alone. Within one epoch the detector breaks on real broadcast frames. The table gives AP50 at 1280 px on three Ekstraklasa clips kept out of training, so it is indicative only.

Second round: changes to the final pipeline

None qualified, so the pipeline did not change and the games kept for confirmation stayed unread.

The tracker already recovers most of what a better detector adds. The crop second stage lifts single frames from 51.6% to 62.4% within 5 m (+10.8 pp [+8.0, +14.0]), but only +1.4 pp survives tracking.

At 1.3 ms per frame it replaces tiling as the single-frame option. The hard negatives help, +2.26 pp [+0.92, +3.72] against the same recipe without them, but the size filter already removes nearly all referee confusions.

The test split was used once before, to choose the detector, so it is not a fresh set. It is also easier for the baseline than the validation split, which explains the smaller gain.

Placements more than 5 m off rise slightly on the test split, from 7.7% to 9.2% of placed balls. Interpolation causes it. One interpolated position in four is more than 5 m off, so the pipeline flags interpolated positions.

Cost is 19.6 ms per frame for detection on the GPU and 0.2 ms for tracking on the CPU.

5  How accurate below 5 m

How precise is a placed ball? Its median error, {{acc_phase1_p50_m}} {{acc_phase1_p50_m_ci}}, is close to the {{acc_reference_centre_p50_m}} median disagreement inside the GSR reference itself.

On the two selection games the pipeline places the ball within 1 m in {{acc_phase1_le1_all}} {{acc_phase1_le1_all_ci}} of frames, within 2 m in {{acc_phase1_le2_all}} and within 5 m in {{acc_phase1_le5_all}}. {{acc_phase1_no_position}} have no position. The 90th percentile error is {{acc_phase1_p90_m}} {{acc_phase1_p90_m_ci}}.

Sub-metre numbers measure agreement with GSR, not physical accuracy. GSR's own ball box, projected with our homography, lies a median {{acc_reference_centre_p50_m}} from GSR's annotated pitch position.

Only 53% of frames with a homography agree within 1 m. For a ball in the air the median is 2.2 m.

Interpolation is the main source of large errors. 25.7% of interpolated positions are more than 5 m off, against 9.8% of measured ones.

Shorter gaps are a poor trade. Cutting the maximum gap from 2 s to 0.5 s removes 3.8 pp of frames placed more than 5 m off, and 7.1 pp of well-placed ones.

A logistic uncertainty score on track features ranks positions better than gap length. With 10% rejected, 10.6% of the rest are more than 5 m off, against 12.1% (−1.5 pp [−2.2, −0.9], fitted on one game, scored on the other).

A threshold chosen on one game cost the other up to 12.4 pp of well-placed frames. The pipeline therefore reports the score per position and does not filter on it.

The size filter is robust to resolution. Scaled with the frame, it removes no true balls at 1080p, 720p or 540p and cuts placements more than 5 m off by 6.1–6.3 pp. A relative-size filter is no better.

The main limitation is that generalisation beyond GSR is unmeasured, because every ball-labelled broadcast set available to us was used for training, selection or an earlier reading.
