Ball localisation in broadcast football video

Jakub Eichner · TactiFoot Vision · research summary, October 2026

The ball is the hardest object to localise in broadcast football. It is often smaller than 12 pixels, blurred, hidden by players or in the air. In our expected-goals pipeline it became the remaining bottleneck once player detection and pitch registration were accurate. This study measures how often the ball can be placed correctly on the pitch, in metres, from broadcast frames, finds where a strong single-frame detector fails, and tests remedies one at a time under a pre-registered protocol. Temporal tracking that reasons in pitch coordinates gives the largest gain. Higher resolution and fine-tuning do not.

1  Problem, data and measure

Measure. The question downstream models ask is where the ball is on the pitch. The measure is therefore the share of frames in which the predicted ball lies within 5 m of the annotated ball after projection with the frame's homography, rather than box AP. A missed ball, a ball on the wrong object and a frame without a homography all count as failures. Data. SoccerNet Game State Reconstruction (GSR): 30-second broadcast clips at 25 fps with annotated ball positions and camera calibration. The validation split has 58 clips from 3 games and the test split 49 clips from 3 other games. Baseline. Our retrained people-and-ball detector (YOLO11l at 1280 px, ball AP@50 0.46). Its single most confident ball per frame is the reference every remedy is paired against. Protocol. Variants are chosen on two validation games and confirmed once on the third. Each experiment has one primary comparison, written down before its result is read. CIs are 95% bootstrap intervals that resample whole clips.

2  Where a single-frame detector fails

Half of all misses are balls smaller than 12 px. Motion blur explains only 19%. The largest source of false balls is a class confusion: referees and assistants in black kits are detected as “ball”. They cause 52% of confident false balls and 55% of placements more than 5 m off. Their boxes are 46–141 px long, while a true ball is never longer than 43 px, so a size filter at 45 px removes them at no cost. On its own it halves the share of placements more than 5 m off (18.5% → 9.6%).

3  What was tested

More pixels alone do not help: at 1920 px the detector finds more balls but also more confident false ones, so its top choice is wrong as often as before. Fine-tuning on oversampled hard examples changed nothing (+0.3 pp). A fine-tuning epoch without mosaic augmentation on a restarted learning-rate schedule cost 8.5 pp, which is a useful warning about fine-tuning recipes. Tiles with the size filter gain +5.3 pp on single frames, but add nothing once tracking is used. The recommended use is therefore tiles for isolated frames (for example the moment of a shot) and tracking for video.

The test split had been used once before to choose the detector, so it is not a fresh set. It is easier for the baseline than the validation split, which explains the smaller gain. Placements more than 5 m off rise slightly on the test split (7.7% → 9.2% of placed balls). The cause is interpolation: one interpolated position in four is more than 5 m off, so interpolated positions are flagged for downstream use. Cost: 19.6 ms per frame for detection on the GPU and 0.2 ms for tracking on the CPU.

6  Open questions

  • Effect on expected goals. The shot location and the ball's state before the shot depend on this component. How much the better ball improves the xG models has not been measured yet.
  • Small balls. Balls under 8 px are invisible to every single-frame variant tested. Candidates are a high-resolution detection head, a second-stage classifier on crops, or training at higher resolution.
  • Hard negatives. Referees in black kits should be fixed in training rather than by a filter. A corrected fine-tuning recipe with these negatives is the next experiment, together with synthetic data for blurred balls.
  • A fresh test set. Both GSR splits have now been read. Confirming the final numbers needs games no decision has seen.
Data: SoccerNet Game State Reconstruction (Somers et al., CVPR-W 2024) and SoccerNet Tracking (Cioppa et al., CVPR-W 2022), used for research only. Unless stated otherwise, CIs are 95% bootstrap intervals resampling whole clips.
