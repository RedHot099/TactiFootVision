Ball localisation in broadcast football video

Jakub Eichner · TactiFoot Vision · research summary, October 2026

The ball is the hardest object to localise in broadcast football. It is often smaller than 12 pixels, blurred, hidden by players or in the air. In our expected-goals pipeline it looked like the remaining bottleneck once player detection and pitch registration were accurate. This study measures how often the ball can be placed correctly on the pitch, in metres, from broadcast frames, finds where a strong single-frame detector fails, and tests remedies one at a time under a pre-registered protocol. Temporal tracking that reasons in pitch coordinates gives the largest gain. Higher resolution, fine-tuning, a second-stage classifier and tracker refinements add nothing measurable on top of it. Below about 1 m the measure is limited by the noise of the reference annotation. For the shot location in expected goals, the shooter's feet remain the better input: no ball variant improves the model.

1  Problem, data and measure

  • Measure. Downstream models need to know where the ball is on the pitch, so the measure is not box AP. It is the share of frames in which the predicted ball lies within 5 m of the annotated ball after projection with the frame's homography. A missed ball, a ball on the wrong object and a frame without a homography all count as failures.
  • Data. SoccerNet Game State Reconstruction (GSR): 30-second broadcast clips at 25 fps with annotated ball positions and camera calibration. Validation: 58 clips from 3 games. Test: 49 clips from 3 other games.
  • Baseline. Our retrained people-and-ball detector (YOLO11l at 1280 px, ball AP@50 0.46). Its single most confident ball per frame is the reference every remedy is paired against.
  • Protocol. Variants are chosen on two validation games and confirmed once on the third. Each experiment has one primary comparison, written down before its result is read.
  • Uncertainty. 95% bootstrap intervals that resample whole clips.

2  Where a single-frame detector fails

Half of all misses are balls smaller than 12 px. Motion blur explains only 19%. The largest source of false balls is a class confusion: referees and assistants in black kits are detected as “ball”. They cause 52% of confident false balls and 55% of placements more than 5 m off. Their boxes are 46–141 px long, while a true ball is never longer than 43 px, so a size filter at 45 px removes them at no cost. On its own it halves the share of placements more than 5 m off (18.5% → 9.6%).

3  What was tested

More pixels alone do not help: at 1920 px the detector finds more balls but also more confident false ones. Fine-tuning on oversampled hard examples changed nothing (+0.3 pp), and an epoch without mosaic augmentation on a restarted schedule cost 8.5 pp.

Two instructive negatives: tiling and synthetic data

Tiling. Four overlapping 1024×608 tiles, run at 1024 px, are merged with the full 1280 px frame; people come from the full frame and the 45 px filter is applied after the merge. On single frames this helps: on the held-out game it adds +5.29 pp [+3.15, +8.02] (77.1% → 82.4%), on the whole validation split +6.03 pp [+4.71, +7.39], and ball AP50 rises from 0.356 to 0.397. Without the filter, tiles lose 0.73 pp on the two selection games: the extra resolution mostly adds large false balls, and the filter is what makes tiling work. On top of tracking the gain does not hold. On the held-out game tiles change the tracked result by −1.25 pp [−2.79, +0.24] and add placements more than 5 m off (+2.48 pp [+1.15, +4.08]); on the selection games they add +4.78 pp [+1.55, +8.39]. The tracker already recovers most of the weak or missed balls that tiles find, and it then follows the extra false balls they bring. Tiles also cost 3.8× the detection time (75–87 ms per frame against 19.6 ms), and balls under 8 px stay missed (recall about 0.06). Tiling is therefore a single-frame mode, not part of the video pipeline. The crop second stage below later filled the same role for 1.3 ms per frame.

Synthetic data. SoccerSynth has 50,877 rendered images with only the ball and people labelled, so the goalkeeper and referee classes were masked out of the classification loss. Fine-tuning a real-data checkpoint on SoccerSynth alone breaks the detector on real broadcast frames within one epoch (AP50 at 1280 px on three Ekstraklasa clips kept out of training, so indicative only):

Tiling: method and result

The frame is split into four overlapping tiles, each run through the detector and merged with the full-frame pass, and the 45 px size filter is applied after the merge. On single frames this finds more balls (about +5 pp within 5 m), but once tracking is used the gain is gone, and it costs 3.8× the detection time. Tiling is therefore only a single-frame mode.

Synthetic data: method and result

SoccerSynth consists of rendered matches in which only the ball and people are labelled. A detector trained on real data was fine-tuned on these renders alone. The ball and the goalkeeper and referee roles collapse on real broadcast frames within one epoch, so synthetic data alone is unusable; a small share mixed into real data was not tested.

It remains an option for isolated frames. The hard negatives work (+2.26 pp [+0.92, +3.72] against the recipe without them), but the size filter already removes nearly all referee confusions.

5  How accurate below 5 m

On the two selection games the final pipeline places the ball within 1 m in {{acc_phase1_le1_all}} {{acc_phase1_le1_all_ci}} of frames, within 2 m in {{acc_phase1_le2_all}} and within 5 m in {{acc_phase1_le5_all}}; {{acc_phase1_no_position}} have no position. Among placed frames the median error is {{acc_phase1_p50_m}} {{acc_phase1_p50_m_ci}} and the 90th percentile {{acc_phase1_p90_m}} {{acc_phase1_p90_m_ci}}.

The sub-metre numbers measure agreement with GSR, not physical accuracy. Projecting GSR's own ball box with our homography and comparing it with GSR's annotated pitch position gives a median disagreement of {{acc_reference_centre_p50_m}}, and only 53% of frames with a homography agree within 1 m. For a ball in the air it is 2.2 m.

Interpolation is the main source of large errors. 25.7% of interpolated positions are more than 5 m off, against 9.8% of measured ones. Shorter gaps are a poor trade: cutting the maximum gap from 2 s to 0.5 s removes 3.8 pp of frames placed more than 5 m off and 7.1 pp of well-placed ones. A logistic uncertainty score on track features ranks positions better than gap length: with 10% rejected, 10.6% of the rest are more than 5 m off, against 12.1% (−1.5 pp [−2.2, −0.9], fitted on one game, scored on the other). But a threshold chosen on one game cost the other up to 12.4 pp of well-placed frames, so the score is exposed per position, not as a filter.

The size filter is robust to resolution. Scaled with the frame, it removes no true balls at 1080p, 720p or 540p and cuts placements more than 5 m off by 6.1–6.3 pp. A relative-size filter is no better.

6  Does a better ball help expected goals?

The xG model places a shot at the feet of the player closest to the ball; against StatsBomb this cut the median location error from 2.62 m (projected ball) to 1.16 m. Three ball variants were refitted out of fold on 7,062 shots from 328 development matches and paired against the feet (log-loss 0.3314).

7  Open questions

  • No untouched ball-labelled data is the main limit. Every ball-labelled broadcast set available to us was used for training, selection or an earlier reading. SoccerNet Tracking (SNMOT) shares its clips with GSR, and SoccerTrack v2 has no ball boxes in the image. Other competitions and cameras are untested.
  • Where the ball matters. For shot location the shooter's feet are enough. The ball is more promising for action analysis (passes, tackles, possession); this is untested.
  • Synthetic data as a small share. SoccerSynth mixed into real data, rather than used alone, was planned but not run.
  • Small balls. Under 8 px, 35% are within 5 m; the 1600 px gain (+8.2 pp [−1.1, +16.1]) rests on 146 frames.
