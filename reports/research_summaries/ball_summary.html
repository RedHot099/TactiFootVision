Ball localisation in broadcast football video

Jakub Eichner · TactiFoot Vision · research summary, October 2026

How often can a broadcast frame place the ball on the pitch, within 5 m of where it is? The ball is the hardest object in the scene. It is often under 12 pixels, blurred, hidden by players or in the air.

We find where a strong single-frame detector fails and test one remedy at a time, under a protocol fixed before results are read. On the GSR test split the share of frames with the ball within 5 m rises from {{base_test}} for the old detector to {{test_track}}. Retraining the detector gives most of this, and tracking in pitch coordinates the rest.

Higher resolution, fine-tuning, a second-stage classifier and tracker refinements add nothing measurable on top of it. Below about 1 m the measure is limited by noise in the reference annotation.

1  Problem, data and measure

The question is where the ball is on the pitch, in metres. Figure 1 shows the pipeline that answers it best.

  • Measure. The share of frames whose predicted ball lies within 5 m of the annotated ball, both projected with the frame's homography. A missed ball, a ball on the wrong object and a frame without a homography all count as failures.
  • Data. SoccerNet Game State Reconstruction (GSR) has 30-second broadcast clips at 25 fps with ball positions and camera calibration. Validation has 58 clips from 3 games, test 49 clips from 3 other games.
  • Detectors. The baseline is the old detector the project started from: YOLO11m trained at 640 px on 1,259 Roboflow football_yolo images, run here at 1280 px. The retrained detector is YOLO11l at 1280 px on larger data, with ball AP@50 0.46.
  • Protocol. We choose variants on two validation games and confirm once on the third. Each experiment has one primary comparison, fixed before its result is read. CIs are 95% bootstraps over whole clips.

2  Where a single-frame detector fails

Where does a strong single-frame detector fail? Mostly on small balls, and on referees in black that it calls “ball”.

Half of all misses are balls smaller than 12 px. Motion blur explains only 19%.

Referees and assistants in black kits cause 52% of confident false balls and 55% of placements more than 5 m off. Their boxes are 46–141 px long. A true ball is never longer than 43 px.

A size filter at 45 px therefore removes them at no cost. On its own it halves the share of placements more than 5 m off, from 18.5% to 9.6%.

3  What was tested

Which remedy places more balls? Temporal tracking. No other change improved the video pipeline.

Each approach was paired against one of two references. The retrained detector means its top-1 ball per frame, 51.6% within 5 m on the two selection games. The final pipeline is tracking plus the size filter, 71.2% [64.6, 77.5] there. Gains are in the share of frames within 5 m, on the selection games unless the held-out game is named.

Higher input resolution. At 1920 px the detector finds more balls, but also more confident false ones, for −0.21 pp [−2.87, +2.10] against the retrained detector. Training and inference at 1600 px gave +0.40 pp [−1.60, +2.35] against the final pipeline.

Fine-tuning. Oversampling small and blurred balls ×2 gave +0.26 pp [−0.80, +1.35] against the same fine-tuning without it. A recipe with referees in black as hard negatives gave −0.61 pp [−2.00, +0.69] against the final pipeline, whose size filter already removes those referees.

Size filter. Ball boxes longer than 45 px are dropped, which removes the referees of Section 2. Against the retrained detector it adds +0.64 pp [+0.12, +1.18] and halves placements more than 5 m off, from 18.5% to 9.6%.

Tiling. The detector also runs on four overlapping 1024×608 tiles, merged with the full 1280 px frame before the size filter. On single frames it adds +5.29 pp [+3.15, +8.02] on the held-out game, against the retrained detector.

On top of tracking it gives −1.25 pp [−2.79, +0.24] on the held-out game, against tracking alone. It also costs 3.8× the detection time.

Synthetic data. SoccerSynth has 50,877 rendered images with only the ball and people labelled. Fine-tuning a real-data checkpoint on it alone drops ball AP50 from 0.161 to 0.004 within one epoch, on three Ekstraklasa clips (indicative).

The goalkeeper and referee classes collapse too. A small share of SoccerSynth mixed into real data was not tested.

Temporal tracking. A tracker in pitch coordinates keeps candidates that fit the ball's motion and interpolates gaps of up to 2 s. Against the retrained detector it adds +17.32 pp [+11.86, +22.82], and +9.15 pp [+4.24, +14.58] on the held-out game.

Tracker refinements. Four changes were tried together: interpolation in the image, a search corridor along gaps, no bridging across restarts, and candidates kept on the penalty and centre spots. Against the final pipeline they gave +0.78 pp [−1.21, +4.12].

Bottom of the box. Projecting the bottom of the ball's box instead of its centre gave −0.68 pp [−1.03, −0.37] against the final pipeline.

Crop second stage. A ResNet18 rescores each candidate on a crop and refines its centre, for 1.3 ms per frame. On single frames it adds +10.80 pp [+7.99, +13.97] against the retrained detector, from 51.6% to 62.4%.

Before tracking it gives +1.41 pp [−0.54, +3.34] against the final pipeline.

The adopted pipeline is temporal tracking plus the size filter (Figure 1). Tiling and the crop second stage remain options for single frames, where the tracker has no neighbouring frames.

The test split was used once before, to choose the retrained detector, so it is not a fresh set. It is also easier than the validation split, which explains the smaller gains there.

For the old detector {{base_far_test}} of placed balls are more than 5 m off on test. The pipeline cuts this to 9.2%, slightly above the retrained detector's 7.7%, because one interpolated position in four is more than 5 m off.

Cost is 19.6 ms per frame for detection on the GPU and 0.2 ms for tracking on the CPU.

5  How accurate below 5 m

How precise is a placed ball? Its median error, {{acc_phase1_p50_m}} {{acc_phase1_p50_m_ci}}, is close to the {{acc_reference_centre_p50_m}} median disagreement inside the GSR reference itself.

On the two selection games the pipeline places the ball within 1 m in {{acc_phase1_le1_all}} {{acc_phase1_le1_all_ci}} of frames, within 2 m in {{acc_phase1_le2_all}} and within 5 m in {{acc_phase1_le5_all}}. {{acc_phase1_no_position}} have no position. The 90th percentile error is {{acc_phase1_p90_m}} {{acc_phase1_p90_m_ci}}.

Sub-metre numbers measure agreement with GSR, not physical accuracy. GSR's own ball box, projected with our homography, lies a median {{acc_reference_centre_p50_m}} from GSR's annotated pitch position.

Only 53% of frames with a homography agree within 1 m. For a ball in the air the median is 2.2 m.

Interpolation is the main source of large errors. 25.7% of interpolated positions are more than 5 m off, against 9.8% of measured ones.

Shorter gaps are a poor trade. Cutting the maximum gap from 2 s to 0.5 s removes 3.8 pp of frames placed more than 5 m off, and 7.1 pp of well-placed ones.

A logistic uncertainty score on track features ranks positions better than gap length. With 10% rejected, 10.6% of the rest are more than 5 m off, against 12.1% (−1.5 pp [−2.2, −0.9], fitted on one game, scored on the other).

A threshold chosen on one game cost the other up to 12.4 pp of well-placed frames. The pipeline therefore reports the score per position and does not filter on it.

The size filter is robust to resolution. Scaled with the frame, it removes no true balls at 1080p, 720p or 540p and cuts placements more than 5 m off by 6.1–6.3 pp. A relative-size filter is no better.

The main limitation is that generalisation beyond GSR is unmeasured, because every ball-labelled broadcast set available to us was used for training, selection or an earlier reading.
